02 / Problem + hypothesis personas

Clarity before complexity.

Financial interfaces become risky when they imply a live balance, silently prefill history, or make actions hard to reverse.

01

Problem

Balances and activity need clear provenance, predictable correction and a hierarchy that can be scanned quickly.

02

Proto-persona · Aisha

Early-career freelancer tracking uneven income. Needs fast manual entries, confidence about what is recorded, and easy correction.

03

Proto-persona · Rohan

Student sharing everyday expenses. Needs quick peer-transfer records, searchable history and privacy when using a shared screen.

These are hypothesis personas created for design coverage, not claimed interview participants. They should be validated with real users before product decisions.

03 / Scope and constraints

A useful demo with honest boundaries.

In scope

  • Start with a truthful ₹0 state and no invented entries.
  • Record income and expenses by category and date.
  • Show search, filtering, editing and deletion flows.
  • Provide privacy visibility toggle and CSV export.
  • Adapt the hierarchy to 390px mobile and desktop.

Out of scope

  • Actual money transfers, bank linking, lending or KYC.
  • Real balances, cards, transaction feeds or payments.
  • Cloud sync, identity/authentication and reconciliation.
  • Claims of completed customer interviews or A/B tests.

Constraint drives the design: this is a local-first money journal, not a regulated banking product.

04 / Journey map

First use, without pretending there is history.

Open wallet→See ₹0 + empty state→Add ₹500 income→Record ₹125.25 outflow→Review activity

Potential concern

“Where did this balance come from?”

Response: label it “Recorded balance” and explain entries stay on this device.

Potential correction

“I entered the wrong amount.”

Response: visible edit/delete actions, protection against impossible negative balances, and a deletion confirmation.

Sequence is an illustrative task scenario used for design verification, not a documented participant journey.

09 / Validation and iteration

Evidence we actually have.

Automated browser checks

  • Wallet input, arithmetic and edit/delete flows.
  • Checkout basket, discount, validation and confirmation.
  • Responsive states at several viewport widths.
  • CI screenshots and repeatable Playwright tests.

Open quality risks

  • No moderated usability interviews or production analytics.
  • Accessibility checks still benefit from hands-on assistive-tech review.
  • Figma prototype links show representative states, not a real payment workflow.
  • A production bank would need stronger storage, privacy and regulatory controls.

Do not interpret passing automated checks as proof of usability or security certification.

10 / Outcome and next steps

Small scope. Complete, reviewable decisions.

The result is a functioning local-first journal, a separate editable checkout exercise, and a shared Figma visual language with linked state demonstrations.

Delivered
